2026 F1 Midseason Review

A Diagnosis of the Season

Posted by Yeeva Tsewang on September ##, 2026

The 2026 Formula 1 season has rounded the halfway point, and so far, these new regulations have proven to unlike anything we've seen before, both in terms of racing dynamics and the conflict of the interest between the driver and an algorithm over who is deciding control of the car. These two talking points have arisen because of the new 50/50 power split between the internal combustion engine and the electric motor. While a 50/50 split may sound promising at first, there were warning signs very early on. Namely, back in 2023 at the Austrian Grand Prix, Max Verstappen warned us about the 2026 cars' driving dynamics from his simulator runs, such as downshifting on the straights. He also noted the diminishing role that the driver plays in operating the car, highlighting how mechanisms like the active aero are outside the control of the driver. These issues indeed plagued the season early on, and still linger. To truly understand the root of these issues, we first need to understand how the ERS (Energy Recovery System) works on an F1 car and its recharge + deployment mechanics. We will then examine the teams and drivers which have adapted the best to these new rules.

Examining the Root

The ERS is the system responsible for the harvesting and deploying of electrical energy. It consists of the MGU-K (Motor Generator Unit-Kinetic), the CU-K (Control Unit-Kinetic), and the ES (Energy Store). Historically, the system also included the MGU-H and CU-H, but the 2026 regulations dropped them.

The MGU-K has two modes: a generator mode and a motor mode. In generator mode, the MGU-K takes kinetic energy from the rear wheels and converts it to electrical energy through the interaction between rotors and their surrounding wire coils in the unit. The spinning tires cause the rotor to spin. This spinning induces an electric current in the unit's wire coils, and this current flows in the opposite direction that the rotor is moving in. Now, the kinetic energy from the wheels need to overcome the electromagnetic forces which want the rotor to move in the other direction. This induces an electric current, which is then stored in the ES. Energy will be captured during braking, lifting-and-coasting, and partial throttle application through corners. In motor mode, the MGU-K does the opposite: it takes the energy stored in the ES to spin the crankshaft and, subsequently, the rear wheels.

The MGU-K can take no more than 8.5MJ of kinetic energy from the brakes during a single lap and can deploy no more than 4MJ per lap. When in Overtake Mode, an additional 0.5MJ can be harvested and deployed. This leads to a full 350kW deployment of only ~11.5 seconds before the battery is drained. Of course, the energy deployment will not be at 350kW across the entire lap. Much of the deployment will be in small, partial bursts at calculated sectors determined by the car's engine mapping. This mapping also determines the optimal times to generate. It's crucial to maintain a balance between deployment and harvesting. On one end, you can deploy your battery to overtake an opponent, only to run out of electric charge down the straight and get re-passed. On the other end, if you constantly harvest but never deploy, then you're certainly going to be slower than a driver who is deploying. Add on top of these the phenomenon of "super clipping," which is when a driver is at full throttle, but the MGU-K switches into generator mode, thus sucking away some of the combustion engine's power and redirecting it from the crankshaft, and you have a mountain of complexity.

Who Has Come Out on Top?

Pre-season testing hinted at the strength of the Mercedes package, and now, over halfway through the season, their advantage is immensely clear. Starting from the season-opener in Melbourne, they've taken the lead in the standings and have held onto it since. They've won nine of the first 13 races and have still stood on the podium when they haven't won. The points data also highlight the impressive performance of Kimi Antonelli, who is currently 66 points ahead of his teammate George Russell, who is second in the standings. Russell, who took an early lead after Melbourne, has had a shaky run so far, due to being hampered by reliability and operational issues. Despite this, the team hasn't brought a whole lot of upgrades so far. Therefore, whenever they do bring a significant upgrade, perhaps many of these wrinkles can be ironed out.

Ferrari has made incredible leaps from last season and now sits second in the standings. They have also maintained their points lead, despite a jump by McLaren after their Miami upgrade. Ferrari themselves brought a power unit upgrade in Austria which saw then further widening the gap between them and McLaren, following strong performances at Silverstone and Spa. A recurring bottleneck at the team seems to be not optimizing their strategy and execution, such as at Monaco and Hungary, where it seemed like a win was within reach. In terms of their drivers, Hamilton has climbed the standings and now sits only 10 points behind Russell. Leclerc, who saw early momentum, now sits at fifth in the standings, 36 points behind his teammate. However, though he had some performance woes starting from Canada through Austria, he bounced back with a win at Silverstone. Since then and up until his DNF at Monza, he has been steadily posting good race results.

McLaren, the defending champions, had a shaky start to the season and was plagued early on with DNFs and DNSs. Chief among the causes is the Mercedes power unit. However, car development seems to be back on the right track, with Norris taking wins at Hungary and Zandvoort, passing Leclerc in the standings. He now sits at fourth in the standings, 16 points ahead of Leclerc. Following outings at Zandvoort and Monza which saw Piastri finally posting some decent points on the board, McLaren now sit 83 points ahead of Red Bull. At this stage, what's crucial for McLaren is to continue developing the car and for Piastri to continue posting points to accompany his teammate.

To round off the "Big Four," we have Red Bull. This year was always going to be a big step for the Milton Keynes based squad since their engine this year was built in-house by Red Bull Powertrains. Suprisingly, the combustion engine is their car's strongest aspect. On the other hand, they've had aerodynamic woes, such as Verstappen's rear wing issues in Austria and Silverstone, which also bled into performance at Spa. On the positive side, up until his injury and temporary departure, Isack Hadjar has been posting consistent points on the board, ensuring that the team doesn't only need to rely on Verstappen. What's critical for Red Bull currently is to continue to make improvements in their aerodynamics and reliability in order to complement their strong engine.

Regarding the midfield, the two that come out on top are Racing Bulls and Alpine.
