Power Unit Pick-Me-Up

How Much of Max Verstappen’s Pitlane-To-P3 Comeback at Interlagos Was Due to the New Engine? Did Red Bull Open Pandora's box?

Posted by Yeeva Tsewang on November 22, 2025

Interlagos once again gave us an absolute masterclass of driving by Max Verstappen, who finished P3 after starting the race from the pitlane due to extensive changes being made on his car under Parc Ferme conditions. The most significant change was the installation of a new Honda power unit.

In a season, Formula 1 teams are only allowed to use a certain number of engines. For the 2024 and 2025 seasons, the amount is four. This means that each engine needs to last for around five to six races. If a driver goes over this allotted amount, they receive grid penalties. However, the benefit to replacing the car's power unit is that it can be run on a more aggressive setting since it doesn't need to last as long, the result being performance benefits.

This is where the line gets muddied.

Current regulations do not specify when engine changes need to be included in a team's cost cap spending and when they don't. They only state that teams need to have good reasoning for engine changes if they are to not be included. Understandably, Red Bull's action at Interlagos raised McLaren's eyebrows, who believes that engine changes for performance benefit should come out of a team's cost cap. The FIA's clarification on this matter is crucial since if Red Bull is not financially penalized, McLaren could employ the same strategy for their drivers.

However, exactly how much of a performance boost does this provide? How much of a performance boost did this provide Verstappen? Using data from previous years' races where circumstances were the same, we can answer (or attempt to, at least) this question.

2021 Italian GP - Bottas

Mercedes changed Valtteri Bottas's engine before the race on Sunday, sending him to the back of the starting grid. Across all the racing laps which were not impeded by safety car deployments or other slow lap scenarios, his average top speed was 339.54 kph whereas Hamilton's was 337.13 kph. Bottas had a top speed of 359 kph whereas Hamilton had a top speed of 354 kph.

By comparison, the field average top speed was 332.26 kph, and the field median was 332 kph. The field's average speed was 237.87 kph, whereas Bottas's was 239.23 and Hamilton's was 238.15.

The important question now is whether the speed difference between Bottas and Hamilton is significant enough to where we can say that the engine swap had noticeable performance gains. These show us that while Bottas had instances when he achieved higher top speeds and average speeds than his teammate, this advantage was likely overshadowed by Hamilton's pure race pace and racecraft (up until his incident with Max on lap 26). That being said, when Bottas did achieve higher top speeds, he usually did so by a considerable margin. This is displayed on laps 8,9, 16, 17, 21, and 22. Now, it is unclear to say precisely whether these gains were from the new power unit or other circumstances. However, we can make an informed guess. Figures 3 and 4 can help us with this.

Figure 3. A plot of Valtteri Bottas's tire evolution over the course of the 2021 Italian Grand Prix. Figure 4. A plot of Lewis Hamilton's tire evolution over the course of the 2021 Italian Grand Prix.

These figures show us that for these laps (8, 9, 16, 17, 21, 22), Bottas and Hamilton were running the exact same age tire and tire compound. Because of this, we can rule out the tires playing a role in the speed difference. Moreover, since the laps plotted are non-slow laps (meaning that laps behind safety car and pit-out laps are excluded), we can eliminate these as a cause, too.

Therefore, it is highly likely that Bottas's power unit swap gave him that extra speed boost over his teammate in scenarios where Hamilton couldn't outdrive Bottas on pure racecraft. On a high-speed track like Monza, this top speed advantage is greatly beneficial.
