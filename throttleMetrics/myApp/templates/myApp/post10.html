The Anatomy of a Straightaway

A Look at Road America

Posted by Yeeva Tsewang on November 8, 2026

Straightaways may seem simple. After all, they're just straight bits of the race track. However, there's far more complexity than what meets the eye, and a bad run has negative effects that ripple over the course of the lap.

The technicality lies in nailing the corner(s) going into the straight. Let's say you have a 90 degree right-hander going into a long straightaway. There's two components of nailing a corner: speed and line. Regarding speed, if you carry too much speed into the turn, then you'll get a poor exit as you try to retain control of your car. On the other hand, if you enter the corner slower, your car will be more stable, and you'll be able to get on throttle earlier. However, if you enter too slowly, then you're going to lose too much lap time.

Regarding the line you take, if you take an early apex, then you're going to get a fast entry speed, but a slower exit which will compromise your speed on the following straight. On the other hand, if you take a late apex, then although you're going to have a slower entry speed, you're going to be able to get on throttle earlier as the car is more square.

How well you perform here directly affects your performance on the straight, which subsequently affects your overall lap performance. To examine these relationships, I took the 2026 XPEL Grand Prix at Road America. I selected this race because Road America contains two of the longest and fastest straightaways on the IndyCar calendar along with very heavy braking zones.

Analysis

For this analysis, I zoomed in on the straightaway which starts from the 90 degree right-hander turn 3 and leads into a heavy braking zone going into the tight left-hander turn 5.

Let's first look at the relationship between turn 3 and the straightaway. Performance is measured in time in seconds. A line of best fit is overlaid on the graph.

As we can see, there is a strong, positive relationship between how well a driver does from the exit of turn 2 into turn 3 and the turn 3 to 5 straightaway. Namely, as the section time increases for the former, it also increases for the latter. This highlights to drivers one very important thing: a sloppy turn 3 will indeed compromise time on the following straight. Therefore, instead of barrelling into the right-hander with an early apex, drivers should follow a more controlled, geometric or late apex line through the corner.

Next, let's look at the relationship between the straightaway and the subsequent track sections from turn 6's entry to turn 9's entry.

Although not as strong, there is still a positive relationship between the two. As straightaway performance worsens, so too does performance on the medium speed sections from turns 6 to 9. This relationship spotlights the importance of executing turns 3 to 5 properly, as a good execution is associated with positive results in the following corners.

Finally, let's look at the relationship between the straightaway and the overall lap time.

As before, there's a positive relationship between the two, and this one is particularly strong, with a 3.5 second worsening in overall lap time associated with a one second increase in straightaway time. Although there are other variables that factor into lap time, this is still a compelling finding. This further emphasizes the strong association between straightaway and lap time performance.

Conclusion

As we've seen, there's more to straightaways than you might think. In racing, every corner is interconnected to another, and a poor time in one has a cascading effect in the other and across the entire lap. Just like that, one poor corner has compromised your entire lap. In this post, we've seen the role that straightaways play in this relationship. Although they seem simple, they require precise execution in the preceding corner. If not done optimally, they have the power to negatively affect not only the following corners, but one's entire lap.
