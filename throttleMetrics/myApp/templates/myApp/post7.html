LMGTE Pro vs LMGTE Am

What's the Effect of Having More Experienced Drivers?

Posted by Yeeva Tsewang on September 22, 2026

Multi-class racing series such as the FIA World Endurance Championship (WEC) and the International Motor Sports Association (IMSA) SportsCar Championship bring with them a unique racing dynamic where cars of different classes and drivers of different skill level compete at the same place and same time. This leads to fields upwards of 60 cars battling for class wins (or overall win in the case of the top class), all while navigating traffic from other car classes.

Grand Touring (GT) cars are one such class, and they are often split by driver experience level, namely Pro and Am. As the names suggest, the difference between the two categories lies in the skill composition of a team's driver lineup. The FIA does this with a grading system, classifying drivers as bronze, silver, gold, or platinum.

The data I used is from the 2022 24 Hours of Le Mans, which ran the LM Grand Touring Endurance (LMGTE) regulation set for grand tourers. LMGTE Am teams were required to have at least one bronze-rated driver, as well as one rated no higher than silver. This meant that only one driver could be rated gold or higher in the team's line-up. LMGTE Pro teams, however, had no restrictions.

This created an interesting question: for Pro and Am teams using the same cars and controlling for confounding variables, what was the lap time impact of having more experienced drivers? In other words, how much of the performance difference between Pro and Am teams was due to the driver experience gap? Through a regression impact analysis, I attempted to quantify this.

Gathering and Prepping Data

The data for this analysis was obtained from Kaggle. This contained sector and lap time data from WEC between 2012 and 2022 across different teams and classes. To make the analysis easier, I limited the scope to be the 2022 24 Hours of Le Mans. Among the Pro teams, the cars used were the Chevrolet Corvette C8.R, Porsche 911 RSR-19, and Ferrari 488 GTE Evo. The Porsche and Ferrari were also used by the Am teams, along with the Aston Martin Vantage GTE. To simplify analysis, I chose to select only one vehicle model. Between the Porsche and Ferrari, I selected the Porsche because, after looking at the Balance of Performance (BoP) LMGTE table for Le Mans 2022, the Porsche had smaller differences between the Pro and Am specced car. Now that the race is selected and year-, circuit-, and vehicle-specific differences have been taken into account, the regression modeling can start.

Regression Modeling

For this multiple linear regression, I used three models: Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), Lasso, and Elastic Net. The equation I used is below. The predictor we are concerned about is \[\text{skill_Pro}\] To isolate the effects of driver lineup experience, I have added additional covariates to account for confounding variables.

\[ \text{lap_time_s} \sim \text{skill_Pro} + \text{team_no} + \text{lap_number} + \text{driver_stint_no} + C(\text{driver_number}) \]
Variable Meaning Interpretation
skill_Pro Dummy variable for driver ranking Lap-time difference associated with a more experienced driver lineup
team_no A combination of the team name and number Controls for persistent team-level performance differences
lap_number The lap number of the race the lap was completed at Captures lap-to-lap changes from traffic, tire wear, and strategy effects
driver_stint_no What stint the driver was on Controls for stint-specific conditions and driver rotation effects
C(driver_number) The driver number that completed the lap (e.g. the team's 1st, 2nd, 3rd driver) Accounts for driver ordering in a team's lineup

Results

After fitting, the results were as follows:

Model Estimate Confidence Interval (95%)
OLS -7.869 [-13.388, -2.350]
Lasso -7.869 [-13.389, -2.349]
Elastic Net -7.869 [-13.389, -2.349]

Across OLS, Lasso, and Elastic Net, the estimate for skill_Pro remains negative and statistically meaningful. After accounting for team-, lap-number-, stint-, and lineup-specific effects, it's shown that the Pro teams are a best estimate of 7.87 seconds faster than the Am teams. The 95% confidence interval spans from 2.35 to 13.39 seconds faster, meaning that we are 95% confident that the true average lap time saved with a Pro driver lineup is between 2.35 and 13.39 seconds. Thus, the lowest plausible average time saved is 2.35 seconds, and the highest plausible average time saved is 13.39 seconds.
